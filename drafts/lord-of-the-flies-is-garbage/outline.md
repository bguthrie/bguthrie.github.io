# Lord of the Flies Is Garbage

## Thesis

Fiction is not evidence about human nature, and Lord of the Flies is the
canonical case of a pessimistic fable that has been laundered into settled
wisdom about what children are underneath. The book is bad allegory, judged
by the standard allegory is supposed to meet; it is worse still as curriculum,
because canonizing an implausible claim about children and then teaching it
to children, for generations, does real damage. The new adaptation on Netflix
is the occasion to say so out loud.

## Antithesis

Literature legitimately illuminates human nature, and the piece risks
reading as philistine STEM-tribal sniping if it targets "fiction" wholesale.
The sharpest version of the counter: Lord of the Flies was written as a
direct reply to Ballantyne's *The Coral Island* — Golding deliberately
reused the names Ralph and Jack and set out to invert the Victorian
optimism of the 1857 original. The book is literature in dialogue with
literature. Golding also had grounds for his view that didn't come from
thin air; he commanded landing craft at D-Day and at Walcheren, where ten
of twenty-seven assault craft were sunk. He had watched civilized men do
atrocious things at close range. Attacking the book for failing a
sociological test it wasn't taking is a category error, and the damage a
reader will want to attribute to the book probably belongs to the
misreaders, not Golding.

## Synthesis

Golding was free to write the book; we are free to judge it. Two judgments
stack and neither retreats. First, it is bad allegory. Good allegory earns
its resonance by saying something plausible about the world; this one just
asserts its pessimism and relies on the reader's prior agreement. The
claim at its core — that children stripped of adult authority revert to
savagery — has no real-world analog of its own setup, and the real
analog most often cited against it (the six Tongan boys on 'Ata in
1965–66) cuts more against the book than for it, even after the fair
objections about setup are granted. Second, the book has been drafted
into the canon and taught to children as a serious statement about
themselves, for generations. A bad book matters little; a bad book
placed in every fourteen-year-old's hands and treated as wisdom is a
different object, and the implicit empirical claim made by that
placement is false. The author happens to be, arguably, a liberal arts
major himself (BA in Computer Science, minor in ancient history), which
should take the STEM-sniping reading off the table before it can get
started.

## Entry Point

Netflix is airing a new adaptation of Lord of the Flies. That is the
occasion for an obligatory reminder that the source material is
garbage, and not in an affectionate way. The piece opens by naming
the show, then drops in the biographical detail (BA in CS, minor in
ancient history) early enough that the reader can't dismiss what
follows as a tech-bro potshot at literature. The frame is not
"engineers don't read"; the frame is "someone who does read, telling
you the emperor's long-canonized novella is naked."

## Sections

### Good allegory earns its resonance; this one just asserts it

- **Claim:** Lord of the Flies is bad allegory, not because allegories
  must be literal, but because an allegory has to say something
  plausible about the world to do its work. LOTF's central claim —
  that children, absent adult authority, revert to savagery — is
  not plausible. It is asserted.
- **Evidence:** Articulate the aesthetic criterion explicitly:
  *Animal Farm* resonates because there was a Russian Revolution
  with Trotskys and Stalins; *The Crucible* resonates because
  McCarthyism was real. LOTF has no such referent in the world.
  Where a defender might reach for Golding's wartime experience as
  grounding, note the more damning biographical detail: during his
  time as a schoolteacher, Golding literally divided his students
  into two groups and pitted them against each other, and his own
  journals record this as source material for the book. This is
  not allegory floating free of the world; this is fiction built
  on a designed intervention a teacher ran on the children in his
  care. The book has an empirical provenance, and the provenance
  is a small cruel experiment with a sample size of one classroom.
- **Objection:** The book is allegory for something other than
  child behavior — postwar disillusionment, original sin, a reply
  to Ballantyne's *Coral Island*. The literal content is a vehicle.
- **Resolution:** Then choose. If the literal content makes no
  claim about children, stop handing it to fourteen-year-olds as
  a parable about themselves. If it does make a claim, the claim
  has to hold up. Defenders want to cash both checks. And the
  teacher-experiment detail forecloses the pure-imagination
  defense anyway: Golding's own view of children came from an
  uncontrolled intervention of his own design, which is to say,
  from fake evidence.

### When anyone actually looked, the story ran the other way

- **Claim:** The closest real-world case to the LOTF scenario is
  the six Tongan boys shipwrecked on 'Ata from June 1965 to
  September 1966. They survived fifteen months. When one of them
  fell off a cliff, the others nursed him back to health. This
  is not a dispositive refutation — the boys came from the same
  strict Anglican boarding school in Nuku'alofa, shared religion,
  hierarchy, and pre-existing bonds, and they foraged seabirds
  and feral chickens from the ruins of the earlier Kolomaile
  settlement rather than starting from nothing. But that
  concession does not save the book; it weakens it further. The
  truthful version is that LOTF's setup (strangers, no
  institutions, no prior bonds, no infrastructure) is so
  artificial that no real scenario can falsify it. A claim that
  cannot fail is not a claim at all; it is a mood. The
  experimental cousins usually invoked alongside LOTF are also
  cooked: Zimbardo's Stanford Prison Experiment was coached, and
  Sherif ran earlier versions of Robbers Cave in which the boys
  refused to turn on each other and quietly binned the results.
- **Evidence:** Keep this light; the HN audience has seen most
  of it. The 'Ata case via Peter Warner's own account and the
  2020 Guardian piece, not routed through Bregman. Le Texier's
  2018 archival work on Zimbardo (*Histoire d'un mensonge*, and
  his 2019 *American Psychologist* paper) for the SPE debunking;
  Gina Perry's *The Lost Boys* for the Sherif point. One
  paragraph each at most.
- **Objection:** A single counterexample doesn't disprove the
  pattern; kids are often cruel; middle school exists.
- **Resolution:** Nobody is claiming children are angels. The
  claim in the book and in its cultural invocation is much
  stronger: that in the absence of adult authority, children
  *revert* to savagery as a default. Default claims need more
  than one pessimistic fable to support them, and the evidence
  that exists runs the other way or not at all. Everyday cruelty
  at school does not support the island-cannibalism claim; it
  supports the claim that children exist.

### A bad book is nothing; a bad book on the syllabus is something else

- **Claim:** The aesthetic judgment alone would not be worth a
  post. People write bad books constantly. What turns this into
  a polemic is the canonization: LOTF has been required reading
  in English-speaking schools for generations, and the new
  adaptation is the latest re-upping of a fable the culture
  never agreed to retire. The institutional choice to canonize
  makes an implicit empirical claim (that this book tells us
  something important about children), and that implicit claim
  is false.
- **Evidence:** LOTF's tenure on secondary-school reading lists
  since the 1960s; its recurrent cultural afterlife in
  adaptations, op-eds, and casual invocation; the current Jack
  Thorne adaptation for the BBC, running on Netflix in the US,
  as the latest datum.
- **Objection:** Canonization is messy; many canonical texts
  have defensible literary merit even when their empirical
  content is off. We still teach *Huckleberry Finn*; we still
  teach *Heart of Darkness*.
- **Resolution:** Those books are taught with their empirical
  content contested, and the teaching usually centers the
  contest. LOTF is typically taught as though the claim at its
  center were settled wisdom. Fix the pedagogy or drop the book.

### What you tell children about themselves matters

- **Claim:** The ultimate stake is formative. What we tell
  children about themselves becomes part of how they understand
  themselves, and telling them, at fourteen, via a text framed
  as serious literature, that they are latent savages held in
  check only by adults, is a specific and ugly thing to hand
  them. Doing it to generations is worse than doing it once.
- **Evidence:** The argument stands as an adult's judgment about
  what children are told. The author is a parent; the prospect
  of his own children being handed this fable bothers him on
  grounds he can defend without retreating into biography.
- **Objection:** Kids are resilient; a novel isn't going to
  rewrite their self-understanding; this is hand-wringing.
- **Resolution:** The cumulative effect of the canon is not one
  book; it is the unremarked pattern of which books are treated
  as important. LOTF's position in that pattern is the problem.
  A fable that tells children the worst thing about themselves,
  framed as important, earns scrutiny.

## So What?

Fiction is not evidence, and the move to use it as evidence is
lazy even when the fiction is good. LOTF is not good, by the
standard allegory ought to meet, and it is important, by the fact
of its canonization. The combination is doing damage to children
who are told a false thing about themselves at a formative age.
The new adaptation is not the crime; it is the latest reminder
that the crime is ongoing. A reader finishes the piece knowing
that the book they were taught to respect fails a principled
aesthetic test *and* a principled evidentiary one, and with a
sharper instinct to notice when fiction is being smuggled in as
settled knowledge elsewhere.

## Open Questions

- **Decide the ask.** The critic flagged that the piece never
  says what it wants done — stop teaching it, teach it as
  contested, let parents opt out. "Fix the pedagogy or drop the
  book" is on the page but isn't committed to in the landing.
  Pick one before drafting or accept that the polemic's job is
  the indictment, not the remedy.
- **The "liberal arts major" biographical move.** Kept in the
  outline because it neutralizes the STEM-sniping counter, but
  the critic read it as defensive writing dressed as confident
  writing ("the argument holding a shield in front of itself").
  Resolve at draft time: plant it in the first paragraph, bury
  it in a mid-essay aside, or cut it entirely and let the
  argument fight from its own strength.
- **Structural ordering.** Critic recommends inverting the
  outline so the curriculum argument leads and the empirical
  takedown supports, on the grounds that the curriculum move is
  the original contribution and the factual reveals are
  saturated on HN. Current ordering retains aesthetic-first
  because the title is "Lord of the Flies Is Garbage" and the
  polemic wants the aesthetic verdict first. Reconsider at draft
  time if the voice doesn't carry it.
- **Empirical section weight.** Kept deliberately light in this
  revision; the HN audience has seen 'Ata and Zimbardo multiple
  times. Verify at draft time that the gesture is enough and the
  section doesn't feel cursory.
- **The aesthetic criterion ("good allegory must resonate with
  reality").** Accepted as polemical assertion per author's
  call. The draft should state it cleanly and not oversell it
  as derived; it is a standard the polemic is applying, not a
  proof.
- **Netflix / BBC framing.** Netflix has the US distribution
  rights for the BBC production (Jack Thorne, Eleven Film,
  premiered BBC One 8 February 2026). Draft can honestly say
  "Netflix is airing a new Lord of the Flies" without claiming
  Netflix produced it.
