### HN pushback on the 'Ata narrative (from top thread)

Summary of the main skeptical threads in the 463-point 2021 discussion:

- **Priming / selection bias**: The boys came from a "strict Anglican boarding school" (not Catholic as occasionally misremembered in comments, per Wikipedia). They had pre-existing social structures, shared religious values, a hierarchy, and the habit of singing and praying together. Commenters noted this is the opposite of LOTF's premise (strangers thrown together without social scaffolding).
- **Cultural preparedness**: Tongan boys had maritime and survival skills British schoolboys wouldn't have.
- **Sample size**: Six people, one instance. Does not establish a default claim either way.
- **Bregman himself contested**: Commenters cited a review calling out Bregman's reliance on other debunked studies (e.g., Marshall's WWII firing-rate claim), accusing him of "as persistent a bias to positivity as other writers have to negativity."
- **Colonial framing**: RNZ critique that the Peter Warner / Bregman telling centers the rescuer's voice, not the boys'.

**Implication:** The outline's Section 2 resolution ("everyday cruelty at school supports the claim that children exist") dismisses the "kids are often cruel" objection too glibly. The smarter objection isn't "middle school exists"; it's "'Ata boys had prior social bonds and a shared value system, which is exactly what LOTF removes in setup, so you haven't actually run the LOTF experiment." The outline does not address this.

### Factual verification

#### 'Ata shipwreck — key facts

Per Wikipedia 'Ata article:
- **Year**: June 1965 (rescued 11 September 1966)
- **Number of boys**: six
- **Background**: "strict Anglican boarding school in Nukuʻalofa on Tongatapu" (not Catholic; outline doesn't state denomination but any draft should)
- **How they got there**: "stole a 24-foot (7.3 m) boat on short notice and with little preparation, and drifted southwest after being caught in a storm"
- **Survival**: "seabirds, feral chickens, wild taro, and bananas from the ruins of Kolomaile" (note: they benefited from pre-existing settlement ruins on the island — not a pure tabula-rasa case)
- **Rescuer**: Peter Warner, "passing lobster fisherman"
- **Aftermath**: imprisoned on return for stealing the boat

The "food garden" claim in the outline is softer than the Wikipedia account, which mostly describes foraging + existing feral resources + ruins. Outline should verify before asserting "built a food garden."

#### Golding biography

Per Wikipedia `William_Golding`:
- Oxford, Brasenose College, 1930. Started **natural sciences**, switched to English for final two years. B.A. second-class honors 1934.
- Royal Navy 1940, lieutenant, commanded landing craft at D-Day, served at Walcheren (October–November 1944) where "10 out of 27 assault craft that went into the attack were sunk."
- Journal records: "set his students up into two groups to fight each other – an experience he drew on when writing Lord of the Flies."

**Implication:** Golding did literally run a primitive, uncontrolled experiment (pitting schoolboys against each other) and used it as source material. This is germane to the "fiction isn't evidence" argument — and the outline is currently *missing* it. It simultaneously strengthens and complicates the piece: strengthens because it shows Golding was himself trying to argue from a data point that was itself a designed intervention; complicates because a defender could say "Golding was empirical, you're just disagreeing with his data."

#### The Coral Island

- Ballantyne, 1857. Three boys: Ralph, Jack, Peterkin. Optimistic imperial-Christian framing.
- Golding's stated motivation: Ballantyne "rotted to compost" in his mind; "a new myth put down roots." He deliberately used the names Ralph, Jack, (Simon/Peterkin) as a direct inversion.

**Implication:** The outline names this briefly in the Antithesis but doesn't use it in the argument. A defender's strongest move is "LOTF is literature in dialogue with literature; demanding it be true about real children is a category error." The outline's resolution ("then choose — either make the claim or stop handing it to fourteen-year-olds") is good but would be sharper if it acknowledged what the allegory was *actually about* (postwar European disillusionment, reply to Ballantyne's imperial optimism) rather than leaving it abstract.

#### Stanford Prison Experiment — consensus

Per Wikipedia:
- **Thibault Le Texier** (2018 book *Histoire d'un mensonge*, 2019 American Psychologist): "the guards were asked directly to behave in certain ways in order to confirm Zimbardo's conclusions, which were largely written in advance of the experiment."
- **Ben Blum** (2018, Medium): cited in outline. Piece: "The Lifespan of a Lie."
- Zimbardo and warden David Jaffe coached guards. Guard David Eshelman admitted he was acting.
- **Alex Haslam and Steve Reicher** (BBC Prison Study, 2006) — earlier independent challenge.

Outline is correct on Zimbardo. Outline attributes "Sherif quietly binned earlier iterations" to Gina Perry's *The Lost Boys*; this is broadly consistent with that book but the outline should verify the specific wording at draft time.

#### Rutger Bregman — credibility check

- Historian, Utrecht BA/MA. Not a social scientist.
- *Humankind* made NYT bestseller; also criticized substantively.
- Wikipedia notes: "a careless reference to sources (the original book contains no index, though the 2022 English reprinting does)"
- Critic quoted: "the argumentation in the book is characterized by circular reasoning and cherry picking"; "although Bregman's project is well-intentioned, it is poorly executed"

**Implication:** The outline leans on Bregman as the key secondary source for 'Ata and for the Zimbardo/Sherif critique. A draft that takes Bregman's framing uncritically is vulnerable. The HN audience has seen both Bregman and the critiques. The piece should cite primary/original sources (Le Texier, Perry, Warner's own account) alongside Bregman, not *through* Bregman.

### Factual issues found in the draft

1. **"Nursed an injured friend back to health after a fall"** — the Wikipedia 'Ata article contains no mention of anyone falling off a cliff or requiring medical recovery from a fall. The detail is in Bregman's Guardian piece (and Bregman's *Humankind*), where it is described as one of the boys breaking his leg after a fall and the others setting it and nursing him. The draft presents the detail as if it's uncontested ambient fact; the HN audience has seen the Bregman telling *and* critiques of Bregman. Not a factual error, but an unacknowledged reliance on Bregman — the very routing the outline's research note said to avoid.

2. **"Foraging seabirds from the ruins of an earlier settlement"** — partial. Wikipedia's account is richer: "seabirds, feral chickens, wild taro, and bananas from the ruins of Kolomaile." The draft's compression drops the feral chickens and cultivated plants, which are arguably the strongest "this wasn't a tabula-rasa survival scenario" detail for the counter-objection the draft never addresses.

3. **"Golding… divided his students into two groups and set them to fight each other"** — CONFIRMED on Wikipedia. Good. The draft's phrase "in his own journals he recorded" is slightly stronger than Wikipedia's "Golding described"; the journals claim needs a more specific source (probably John Carey's 2009 biography) or should be softened to "Golding described."

4. **"Sherif ran earlier iterations, and in those the boys instead ganged up on the experimenters themselves"** — CONFIRMED on Wikipedia Robbers Cave article, attributed to Cherry (1995). Good.

5. **"Calvinist fever-dream, original sin made manifest destiny"** — this is rhetorical, not factual. Golding was Anglican by upbringing, not Calvinist; the "original sin" reading is a critical commonplace but "Calvinist" is a coloration the draft doesn't defend. The HN pedant class will flag this.

6. **"Walcheren, where ten of twenty-seven assault craft in his flotilla were sunk"** — CONFIRMED on Wikipedia.

7. **"The Nazis didn't get to be Nazis by being stranded on a desert island"** — rhetorical flourish, accurate in the trivial sense, but the sentence is doing less argumentative work than the draft treats it as doing. The question isn't whether fascism has historical causes; it's whether a story about children on an island is a useful way to introduce moral formation. The draft smuggles a switch there.

### Evaluation against the five criteria

**1. Promise delivery — ADEQUATE.**
Opening promises: (a) the book is bad allegory; (b) its canonization does damage; (c) the new adaptation is the occasion. The draft delivers (a) in section 1, (b) partially in sections 2–3, and (c) only in the opening sentence and by implication. What the draft promises and then under-delivers is the pairing the title and opening set up: an aesthetic-AND-evidentiary double verdict. The evidentiary verdict is strong; the aesthetic argument ("good allegory must resonate with reality") is asserted but not worked. The draft tells the reader this is the criterion; it does not convince the reader it is the right criterion. Per the outline's own Open Question note, the criterion was "accepted as polemical assertion" — but the draft leans on it as if it had been earned.

**2. Evidence gaps — WEAK.**
- "Taught as canon to generations" / "for decades" / "one of the most reliably assigned novels in the English-speaking canon" — asserted three times, never dated or cited. Wikipedia doesn't date the canonization and the research file flags it; the draft goes ahead anyway. A single citation (a US Common Core list, a UK AQA GCSE placement, anything) would fix this.
- "The institutional choice to keep teaching it as serious literature makes an implicit empirical claim" — this is the argumentative pivot of the whole piece. It's asserted without defense. *Why* does placing a book on a syllabus imply an empirical claim? The piece needs one clean paragraph on why curriculum placement is doing the work of endorsement rather than, say, "this book is a widely-discussed-enough cultural artifact that students should be able to talk about it." A well-read teacher will push back here; the draft has no reply.
- "The closest anyone has come to the setup, the six Tongan boys… spent fifteen months cooperating" — the draft never addresses the concessions the outline explicitly flagged: the boys came from the *same strict Anglican boarding school*, shared hierarchy and religion, and had pre-existing bonds. The outline's Section 2 Claim was notably more honest than the draft's rendition. Omitting the concessions hands the HN skeptic a free rebuttal the author already had an answer for.
- "Zimbardo was coached; the guards were rehearsed, the warden was Zimbardo himself" — fine, footnoted to Le Texier. Good practice; the only footnote in the piece. Why just one?
- "Mostly, it's about one mid-century English schoolmaster's half-digested Calvinism" — "Calvinism" is asserted; nothing in the draft defends the reading, and Golding's Anglican background sits awkwardly against it.
- The author's own authority (CS degree, ancient history minor) was flagged in the outline as a move to neutralize STEM-sniping — the draft cuts it entirely. Whether that's the right call is a judgment; the absence should be a deliberate choice, not an oversight. The draft substitutes the more general "I have a fair bit of sympathy for the idea that tech would be better off if its most visible practitioners had a better sense of civic virtue," which does the neutralization at a different angle. Defensible; the piece loses a tight personal stake (CS-with-ancient-history-minor is load-bearing in a way generic sympathy-for-civic-virtue isn't).

**3. Entry point — ADEQUATE, bordering on weak.**
Opening: "Netflix is airing a new television adaptation of *Lord of the Flies*. This is a good occasion to remind everyone that the source material is garbage, and always has been."
The first sentence is functional; the second is the thesis. Sentence three ("It is bad allegory masquerading as ethnography; it has been taught as canon to generations of schoolchildren, but the implicit empirical claim its canonization makes about what children _are_ is false") is dense but does work. Sentence four ("I disliked the book as a kid, and I dislike it more now that I have children of my own") earns personal stake. Sentence five ("It sucks") is the tonal wink and probably either earns a nod or an eye-roll depending on the reader.
The second paragraph is the bigger problem. It is a ninety-word apologia for the *rest* of the piece ("I'm not just a tech-sniper, but") before the argument has started. The outline explicitly flagged this as a risk — "the argument holding a shield in front of itself" — and the draft walked into it. A busy HN reader is now three hundred words in without having seen a load-bearing argument. Cut the second paragraph or demote it to a mid-essay aside; the piece is stronger starting from "Allegory, to earn the reader's assent, must say something plausible about the world."

**4. Section discipline — ADEQUATE.**
Four sections:
- "Good allegory earns its resonance; this one just asserts it" — on thesis, good, makes the aesthetic case and introduces the Golding-as-schoolmaster move. The paragraph comparing Zimbardo/Sherif to the book is structurally awkward — those are attacks on *adjacent* studies, not on the book itself, and the section is titled "good allegory." This material belongs in the next section (curriculum / empirical claim) or in its own short section, not embedded in the allegory argument. The transition lands the reader in a different building than the section title promised.
- "You don't get to cash both checks" — strong. This is the sharpest section. The "either/or" framing of the allegory defense is doing the piece's hardest work.
- "What we tell children about themselves matters" — combines two different moves: (a) the formative harm of what you tell a child they are; (b) the claim that history/sociology would be better tools for a similar pedagogical aim. These are related but distinct; running them in sequence makes the section feel like two short essays. The Nazis sentence is vivid but does less work than it looks like it does.

What's not in a section: an affirmative statement of the ask. The outline's Open Question #1 said: decide whether the piece is asking for the book to stop being taught, be taught as contested, or parents to be allowed to opt out. The draft landed on "quit it with this fucking book" — i.e., stop teaching it. That's a defensible choice but a strong one; the penultimate paragraph ("the answer is history, read by people doing their earnest best to work out what actually happened, and sociology and ethnography honest enough to observe people as they are") is the closest the piece comes to an affirmative ask, and it's positioned as an aside.

**5. Coherence — ADEQUATE.**
The logical thread — this book is bad allegory → allegory defenders can't have it both ways → canonization is what makes it matter → what you tell kids about themselves is formative — is visible and mostly runs. Where it breaks:
- The Zimbardo/Sherif paragraph in section 1 is a detour that reads more like "here's my list of also-debunked-studies" than a load-bearing move. It belongs further down, or it needs a sentence tying it back to LOTF specifically.
- The Golding-as-schoolmaster detail is the strongest new material in the piece but it's doing *two* jobs: (i) refuting the "it's just imagination" defense; (ii) attacking Golding's character. The draft runs them together and (ii) starts to crowd out (i). The one-line "self-flattering conclusion from his own behavior" ends section 1 on a note that's rhetorically satisfying but tonally sharper than the argument earns.
- The pivot from "canonization" (section 3) to "what we tell children" (section 4) is a tonal shift from institutional-critique to parental-concern that lands well but blurs whose voice is arguing. The piece is sharper when the "I have kids" voice stays separate from the institutional voice.

### Summary judgment

The draft is a competent 80% of a strong polemic and needs one substantive pass, not a rewrite. The aesthetic-criterion argument is under-defended given its load-bearing position; the curriculum/canonization argument is asserted but not grounded; and the 'Ata rebuttal omits concessions the outline had already worked out, handing skeptics a free win. The second paragraph is defensive writing the outline explicitly warned against.

### Prioritized revisions

1. **Cut or demote the second paragraph.** The "I have sympathy for the idea that tech would be better off" paragraph is the shield-in-front-of-itself the outline flagged. Delete, or move to a mid-essay aside where it doesn't block the reader getting to the argument.

2. **Add the 'Ata concessions the outline already prepared.** The outline's Section 2 ("the boys came from the same strict Anglican boarding school, shared religion, hierarchy, and pre-existing bonds, and they foraged seabirds and feral chickens from the ruins of the earlier Kolomaile settlement rather than starting from nothing") is better than the draft's rendering. Bringing the concessions forward and *then* turning the "claim that cannot fail is not a claim at all" move earns more than asserting it cold.

3. **Ground the canonization claim with one concrete citation.** A single dated placement — e.g. AQA GCSE English Literature, or a state-level US ELA list — closes the largest evidentiary hole in the piece. Without it, "for generations" and "for decades" read as polemic rhythm rather than claim.

4. **Defend the aesthetic criterion for one paragraph.** "Good allegory earns its resonance by saying something plausible about the world" is doing enormous work. A reader primed on *The Trial*, *Waiting for Godot*, *Blood Meridian*, or Borges will want to know why the criterion isn't a category error against *them*. One clean paragraph — "the criterion applies to allegory that is loaded with an empirical claim; pure parables and existential fables are a different case" — closes the flank.

5. **Move or reframe the Zimbardo/Sherif paragraph.** It belongs either (a) after the 'Ata case, as part of a "what actual evidence says" cluster, or (b) in its own short section titled for what it's doing. Embedded in section 1 it's a detour.

6. **Decide the ask explicitly.** The piece lands on "quit it with this fucking book." That's a strong ask; state it as the ask, or soften to "teach it as contested." The outline flagged this as unresolved and the draft left it unresolved.

7. **Replace "Calvinist" or defend it.** "Half-digested Calvinism" is a coloration the draft doesn't earn. Anglican is the safer and more defensible reading. If Calvinist is the right word, give it a sentence.

8. **Trim "Netflix" at first reference to be verifiable.** Wikipedia names BBC One / BBC iPlayer as the premiere venue. If the Netflix US-distribution detail is the frame, a one-clause gesture ("airing via Netflix in the US / BBC in the UK") protects against the pedant objection without breaking the opening.

9. **Consider the CS-minor-in-ancient-history detail.** The outline kept it deliberately and the draft dropped it for a more diffuse sympathy-for-civic-virtue paragraph. The more specific biographical stake was sharper; the generic version is a softer version of the same move. Pick one and commit.
